Serial Position Effect

People best remember the first and last items in a series. The middle gets lost.

First described by Ebbinghaus (1885). Later research split it into the primacy and recency effects.

Source: lawsofux.com/serial-position-effect · Ebbinghaus, 1885

[~3 min] Serial Position Effect. Remember the grid at the start of this module, where the first and last tiles stayed lit? This is why. People remember the first and the last items in a list best, and the middle gets lost. [Point to the chart] This is the classic U-shaped recall curve. On the left, the items at the start get rehearsed more, so they move into longer-term memory. That’s the primacy effect. On the right, the items at the end are still fresh in working memory. That’s the recency effect. And the middle? It gets neither advantage. [Pause] Hermann Ebbinghaus first noticed this in his memory experiments back in 1885, and it’s been replicated a lot since then. One quick caveat, the bar heights here show the shape, they’re not exact data. So what does this mean for us? Position is a priority signal. The most important thing in any list, menu, flow or presentation belongs at the beginning or the end. [Ask the room: “Without looking, what were the first and last laws we covered in Module 1?”] [Let people answer. Most will get Hick’s Law and Selective Attention.] And the middle ones are fuzzier, right? That’s the effect, live. Let’s look at it in the wild.
