Pareto Principle

For many outcomes, roughly 80% of the effects come from 20% of the causes.

Named after economist Vilfredo Pareto, who noticed about 80% of Italy’s land was owned by 20% of its people.

Source: lawsofux.com/pareto-principle · Pareto, 1896

[~3 min] The Pareto Principle. You’ve probably heard it as the 80/20 rule. For many outcomes, roughly 80% of the effects come from 20% of the causes. It’s named after the economist Vilfredo Pareto, who noticed that about 80% of the land in Italy was owned by about 20% of the people. [Point to the chart] Here’s what it usually looks like in a product. Twenty features, ranked from most used to least used. The top four, that’s the top 20%, account for roughly 80% of all the usage. Then there’s this long tail of features people barely touch. [Pause] Now, a caveat. This chart is illustrative, not real data. And the actual split varies. Sometimes it’s 70/30, sometimes 90/10. 80/20 is a pattern, not a law of nature. But that shape shows up everywhere: usage, revenue, bugs, support tickets, page views. So here’s the practical lesson. If most of the value sits in a few places, spreading our effort evenly across everything is a mistake. We should find the vital few and give them a lot more care.
