Recognition beats recall.

The seven-item menu myth

Miller is often cited to cap menus at seven items. But menus are scanned, not memorized.

Scanning isn’t remembering.

Command palettes

VS Code’s command palette lets you type a few letters and pick from matches, no exact command needed.

Show it, don’t quiz them.

[~2 min] Let’s start with the myth, because I’d bet half of us have heard it in a design review. [Point to the left card] “Miller’s Law says no more than seven menu items.” Raise your hand if you’ve heard that one. [Pause for hands] Here’s the problem. Miller studied how many unrelated things people can hold in memory. A navigation menu is sitting on screen the whole time. You’re scanning it, not memorizing it. A long, well-organized menu can be perfectly usable. So where does Miller’s Law actually bite? Anywhere we depend on recall: commands you have to type exactly, codes you have to remember, options we described on a previous screen and expect you to carry forward. [Point to the right card] Command palettes are the fix in action. VS Code’s is a great example. A command line makes you remember the exact command. A palette lets you type a few letters and pick from what matches. That turns recall into recognition. [Ask the room: “Where do we make someone remember something exactly, when we could show it to them?”] [Let two or three people answer.] Quick note: these illustrations are generic sketches, not real screenshots.
