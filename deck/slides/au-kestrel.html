Beat 3 · At Kestrel (fictional)

The gorgeous redesign

Kestrel’s new booking app looked stunning. Testers praised it in every session.

But most of them never finished booking a visit. The praise hid the problem.

The fix: track task success separately from how people say they feel.

What testers said

“Beautiful!”

“So clean.”

“I love the colors.”

“Super easy!”

What testers did

Opened the app

Picked a service

Chose a time

Booked a visit

Bars are illustrative.

[~3 min] This is a story almost every product team lives through at some point. Kestrel, our fictional home-services company, ships a gorgeous redesign of its booking app. And the testing sessions are a lovefest. [Point to the left column] Here’s what testers said: “Beautiful.” “So clean.” “I love the colors.” “Super easy!” Everyone leaves the debrief feeling great. [Pause] [Point to the right column] And here’s what testers actually did. They opened the app, most picked a service, fewer chose a time, and very few actually booked a visit. Those bars are illustrative, but the pattern is real. Both columns are true at the same time. The Aesthetic-Usability Effect made people kinder in what they said than in what they did. [Ask the room: “If you were in that debrief, which column would you have heard about?”] [Let two or three people answer.] Usually it’s the quotes. So the fix here isn’t a design change, it’s a process change. Track task success separately from how people say they feel. And when the two disagree, believe the behavior. Okay, what does that mean for each of us on Monday?
