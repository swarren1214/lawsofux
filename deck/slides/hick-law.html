Beat 1 · The law

Hick’s Law

The more choices you offer, and the harder they are to compare, the longer people take to decide.

Hick and Hyman (1952): decision time grows with the logarithm of the number of options. More choices never come free.

Source: lawsofux.com/hicks-law · Hick, 1952; Hyman, 1953

[~3 min] Okay, Hick’s Law. The short version: the more options you put in front of someone, and the harder they are to compare, the longer it takes them to pick one. This comes from William Hick and Ray Hyman in the early 1950s. [Point to the animation] Watch the right side. Every time a new option appears, the bar gets a little longer. But look at the shape of the curve. It climbs fast at first and then flattens out. That’s logarithmic, and it’s the part people miss. Going from two choices to four costs you more than going from ten to twelve. So the first few options you add are the expensive ones. [Pause] To be clear, the animation is illustrative, not measured data. Now, a caveat before anyone goes back to their team and starts deleting dropdown items. Hick’s Law is about choosing between options you actually have to think about. If you’re picking your state from an alphabetical list, you’re not weighing fifty options, you’re scanning for one you already know. That behaves differently, so don’t use this law to argue against long lists people already know how to scan.
