The power of defaults

Johnson & Goldstein (2003): countries with opt-out organ donation had far higher consent rates.

Defaults are decisions.

[~3 min] These are two of the most cited examples of bias in real, high-stakes decisions. On the left, a 1982 study by Barbara McNeil and colleagues. People were choosing between surgery and radiation for lung cancer. When surgery’s outcomes were described as survival rates, “90% survive,” people favored it a lot more than when the same numbers were described as mortality rates, “10% die.” [Pause] And this is the part that gets me. It wasn’t just patients. Physicians were affected too. Trained experts, same bias. Wording changes decisions. On the right, Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein in 2003. They compared European countries on organ donation. Where donation was the default and you had to opt out, consent rates were far higher than in countries where you had to opt in. Same people, same choice, different default. Defaults are decisions. So here’s what that means for us. Every default we ship and every label we write is a choice-architecture decision, whether we meant it to be or not. These are generic sketches, not the actual study materials.
