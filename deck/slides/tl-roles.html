Beat 4 · Your move

Tesler’s Law on Monday morning.

Product

“Who carries the complexity?”

Decide on purpose whether users or the system absorb it.

Engineering

“Can the system do this?”

Spend the extra week so users don’t each spend extra minutes.

QA

“Where did it move to?”

Test the edge cases the system now handles for users.

Next: Occam’s Razor →

[~2 min] So what do you actually do with Tesler’s Law on Monday morning? Product folks, your question is “Who carries the complexity?” Every feature has some complexity you can’t remove. Make an explicit decision about where it lives. Don’t let it land on users by accident. And when the cost is reasonable, default to the system carrying it. Engineering, yours is “Can the system do this?” Honestly, this is the law that most directly argues for engineering effort. Smart defaults, lookups, inference, validation: those are all ways of moving complexity off users. It helps to actually do the math out loud in planning. One week of engineering versus one minute for every user, every time they hit this screen. QA, your question is “Where did it move to?” Because when complexity moves into the system, the system has to get it right. All the edge cases users used to handle themselves, odd addresses, boundaries, exceptions, are now ours to test. [Ask the room: “QA, what’s an edge case you’ve seen that came from the system doing work on the user’s behalf?”] [Let one or two people answer.] Next up is a principle that pushes in almost the opposite direction: Occam’s Razor.
