A line makes a family.

Transit maps

Connection beats distance.

Checkout steppers

Checkout flows join numbered steps with a line, so four separate screens read as one journey.

Connect what’s sequential.

[~2 min] [Point to the left card] Transit maps. Harry Beck’s London Underground map, first published in 1933, threw away geography almost entirely. The distances are wrong, the angles are simplified. And it works anyway, because every station on a line is joined by one continuous colored stroke. That connection is all a rider actually needs. Connection beats distance. [Point to the right card] Checkout steppers apply the same idea to time instead of space. You’ve got four separate screens, but a line runs through the numbered steps, so they read as one journey. And the filled part of the line shows how far you’ve come. Same caveat as before, these are generic sketches. [Ask the room: “Where do we have a sequence, something people do in order, that isn’t visibly connected on screen?”] [Let two or three people answer.] I’ll give you one from Kestrel that cost them a lot of reopened jobs.
