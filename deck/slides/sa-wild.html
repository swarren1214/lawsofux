Beat 2 · In the wild

Loud isn’t the same as seen.

Banner blindness

Nielsen Norman Group’s eye-tracking studies show people skip anything that looks like an ad.

Don’t dress info as ads.

Car warning lights

Cars put warnings in fixed spots with standard symbols and colors, so drivers catch them without hunting.

Make urgent look urgent.

[~2 min] [Point to the left card] Banner blindness is one of the most replicated findings in web usability. Nielsen Norman Group has seen it over and over in eye-tracking studies. People learn really quickly what ads look like, where they sit and how they move, and their eyes route around them automatically. Here’s the trap for us: if we put important information in a box that looks like a promotion, it inherits that same blindness. It doesn’t matter how important it is. [Point to the right card] Car warning lights are the opposite approach, and I think they’re brilliant. Warnings appear in the same place every time, with standard symbols and colors, and they stay dark when nothing’s wrong. Drivers notice them precisely because they’re rare, consistent and distinct. [Ask the room: “Do we have any warnings that are always on, so people have stopped seeing them?”] [Let two or three people answer.] These are generic sketches, not real screenshots. Now, Kestrel. And this is the most serious example in all of Module 1.
