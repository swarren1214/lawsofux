Chunking

Break information into small, meaningful groups and people can take in far more of it.

Chase & Simon (1973): chess experts recall whole boards by seeing familiar patterns, not single pieces.

Source: lawsofux.com/chunking · Chase & Simon, 1973

[~3 min] Chunking is how we work around the memory limits we just talked about. [Point to the animation] Same digits on the right, but watch what happens when they get grouped. Ten loose digits are ten things to hold. The same digits written as a phone number are three. Nothing was removed. The load just dropped. Quick note: neither number in the animation is real, it alternates a phone number and an example card number. The research behind this is one of my favorites. Chase and Simon, back in 1973, studied chess experts. Show an expert a real game position for a few seconds and they can rebuild almost the whole board. Show them pieces placed at random, and most of that advantage goes away. They weren’t remembering pieces. They were remembering patterns, chunks they’d seen a thousand times. In our world, chunking shows up as formatting, grouping, headings and white space. [Ask the room: “What’s something you remember easily only because it’s grouped, like a phone number?”] [Let two or three people answer. Expect things like card numbers, license plates, Social Security numbers.] Good. Let’s look at a couple of examples in the wild.
