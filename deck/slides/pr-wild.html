Airport pictograms

Reduce to the essential.

[~2 min] [Point to the left card] App icons are the everyday example. Think about your home screen right now. It’s crowded, and you still find the app you want in a fraction of a second. That’s because the best icons reduce a whole product to one bold, distinctive shape on a rounded square. One shape, instant recall. [Point to the right card] The classic case study is the AIGA and DOT symbol signs. The US Department of Transportation commissioned them in the 1970s, and the set came out in 1974. Designers stripped people, planes and coffee cups down to the simplest forms that still read, in any language. Versions of them are still in airports all over the world. These are generic sketches, not the actual symbols. [Pause] Here’s the rule I’d take away: if an icon needs a second look, it isn’t simple enough. [Ask the room: “Which of our icons would survive being seen for one second at the end of a hallway?”] [Let a couple of people answer.] Kestrel learned this one the hard way, with bugs. Literal bugs.
