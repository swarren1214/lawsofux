[~2 min] Two examples, and the sketches here are generic, not the actual products. [Point to the left card] First, TV remotes. You all know the cable remote with fifty-something buttons, most of which nobody has ever pressed. The Apple TV remote keeps a handful and moves everything else on screen. And here’s the important part: it didn’t remove any capability. You can still reach every setting. It just doesn’t make you consider all of them every time you want to pause. [Point to the right card] Second, Google’s homepage. In the late nineties, the big portals crammed news, weather, mail and shopping onto the front page. Google gave you one field. There was nothing to decide. Both examples make the same move: they removed choices from the moment of decision, not from the product. So, hide rare choices, don’t delete them, and make the main path the only obvious one. [Ask the room: “Name a product you use every day that does the opposite.”] [Let a few answer. Car infotainment screens and enterprise admin consoles usually come up.] Right. Now let’s go see how Kestrel got itself into this.
