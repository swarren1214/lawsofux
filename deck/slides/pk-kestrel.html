Beat 3 · At Kestrel (fictional)

The blank report

Kestrel’s job report opened with an empty box: “Describe the service in detail.”

With no guidance, techs spent long stretches writing, and reports still varied wildly.

The fix: quick-pick what was done, prefill from the job, and keep a short optional note.

[~3 min] So picture a Kestrel tech at the end of a service visit, standing in the customer’s driveway, trying to finish the job report before heading to the next stop. [Point to the Before card] This is what they got. A big empty box: “Describe the service in detail.” And a Submit button. That’s it. With no guidance, techs spent a long time writing paragraphs. And the reports still varied wildly. Some were three words, some were an essay. Here’s why this is Parkinson’s Law. An empty text box is basically an open-ended deadline. There’s no signal for when the report is good enough, so it grows to fill whatever time the tech gives it. And the quality still isn’t consistent. [Point to the After card] The fix shrinks the task to its minimum. “What did you treat?” Perimeter, Interior, Bait: tap, tap, tap. The products used are filled in from the job record. And the note is still there, but now it’s optional and short. So the reports got faster and more consistent at the same time. Kestrel is fictional, but you can see this happening on any form with a big blank field. [Ask the room: “Which of our tasks are open-ended when they could be quick?”] [Let two or three people answer.] Let’s bring it home for each team.
