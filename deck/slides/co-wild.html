Give choices a clear shape.

[~2 min] Two examples, both generic sketches. [Point to the left card] The jam study again, so we have the numbers side by side. Shoppers crowded around the 24 jams, then walked off. Of the people who stopped at the 6-jam table, about ten times as many bought. The lesson is simple: attention isn’t a sale. A screen that gets lots of looks and few decisions isn’t working. [Point to the right card] Then Apple in 1997. When Steve Jobs came back, Apple had a confusing range of overlapping models. He replaced it with a simple two-by-two grid: consumer or pro, desktop or portable. Anyone could find their computer in a few seconds. [Pause] Here’s the takeaway, and it’s a little subtle. The lesson isn’t “offer fewer things.” It’s “give the choice an obvious shape.” Apple still sold several products. You just never wondered which one was for you. [Ask the room: “If a new customer looked at our pricing or plan page, could they tell which one is for them in five seconds?”] [Take a couple of answers.] Hold that thought, because Kestrel’s about to walk right into it.
